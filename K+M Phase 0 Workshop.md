# K+M Phase 0 Workshop
## Client-Facing Workshop Experience & Presentation Blueprint

### Tender Scout & Innovation Radar

---

# 1. The central design principle

The workshop webpage should not behave like a normal PowerPoint presentation.

It should behave like a **guided decision journey**.

The psychological sequence throughout the workshop should be:

**You told us → We understood it → This is what still needs definition → We decide it together → It becomes part of the confirmed baseline.**

This achieves several things at once:

- K+M immediately sees that their preparation was read carefully.
- We do not look like we are restarting discovery.
- Missing information does not feel like they “failed the questionnaire.”
- Each discussion has a visible reason.
- Decisions create visible progress.
- The meeting ends with something tangible rather than 90 minutes of conversation.

The client should never wonder:

> “Why are we discussing this?”

Every page should make that obvious.

---

# 2. What the workshop must accomplish

The workshop must leave us with an implementation-ready baseline in five areas.

### Tender Scout
We need to know:

**Where do we search?**

**What exactly are we looking for?**

**What should we ignore?**

**How should the system recognize relevance?**

**Who receives the result and what must Release 1 do?**

### Innovation Radar
We need to know:

**Who and what do we monitor?**

**Which developments matter?**

**Which sources/signals should feed the radar?**

**How should information be prioritized?**

**Who receives the result and what must Release 1 do?**

### Cross-project
We must also leave with:

- confirmed business owners
- confirmed sign-off responsibility
- remaining client inputs, if any
- owner for every open item
- clear next steps after Phase 0

Everything else is secondary.

# 4. Client-facing UX

## One decision at a time

This is the most important UX choice.

Do not build one long page containing 25 questions.

Do not recreate the questionnaire.

Do not display giant tables.

Instead:

> One screen = one conceptual decision.

For example:

**Priority geography**

Then:

**Priority tender sources**

Then:

**What makes a tender relevant?**

The participant's brain should always know what they are currently deciding.

# 6. Three workshop statuses

Internally, every questionnaire item belongs to one of three states:

### Confirmed
Enough information already exists.

These should take seconds, not minutes.

### Refine
The answer exists but is too broad for implementation.

These deserve workshop discussion.

### Define together
The questionnaire did not produce an answer.

These require a decision.

Client-facing wording should always be neutral.

Never display:

**Missing information**

Use:

**To define today**

That small wording difference matters.

## Strong supporting question

Ask for examples:

# 15. Tender Scout decision 4
## Keyword logic

Do not simply ask:

> “Any more keywords?”

That produces an arbitrary word list.

Instead turn the current list into a basic relevance taxonomy.

# How should Tender Scout recognize relevant opportunities?

Show the existing words as a **draft grouping for confirmation**.

### Core domain terms
Rail  
Catenary  
Trolleybus  
Light rail  
Tram

### Technical / component terms
Cantilever / Ausleger  
Fahrdraht / Contact Wire  
Overhead Contact Line

Then define three categories:

## Strong relevance signals
Terms that strongly indicate relevance.

## Supporting signals
Terms that increase relevance when appearing with other relevant context.

## Exclusion terms
Terms that may help eliminate false positives.

Then ask:

> Are important synonyms, translations or technical terms missing?

This is much better than trying to create a massive keyword database during the workshop.

### Critical principle

The workshop should establish:

- the **logic**
- the **seed vocabulary**
- the **important languages/synonyms**

It should **not** spend 20 minutes wordsmithing an exhaustive dictionary.

The expanded dictionary can be prepared from the confirmed baseline afterwards and validated with K+M.

# 33. Information density

The client-facing presentation should contain approximately **20–30% of what you actually know and plan to say.**

The remaining 70–80% belongs in:

- your preparation
- your facilitation notes
- follow-up questions
- examples you can use when needed

If everything you plan to say is written on screen, the client stops listening and starts reading.

The screen should answer:

> “What are we deciding?”

You should provide:

> “Why does it matter and how should we think about it?”

# 40. Use examples carefully

Examples are useful only when the client struggles to answer.

If we show examples too early, we risk anchoring them to our ideas.

The sequence should be:

1. Ask the business question.
2. Give them space.
3. Clarify.
4. Only then offer examples if helpful.

The website can therefore contain subtle:

**Need examples?**

which reveals prompts only when clicked.

This is ideal UX.

# 48. Final principle

The webpage is not the product of the workshop.
